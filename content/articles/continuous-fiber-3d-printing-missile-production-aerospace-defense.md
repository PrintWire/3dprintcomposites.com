The U.S. Army's multi-year contract with Continuous Composites puts **continuous fiber 3D printing missile production aerospace defense** work on the record. On June 22, 2026, the Coeur d'Alene, Idaho company announced that the U.S. Army DEVCOM Aviation and Missile Center (AvMC) had awarded it a multi-year contract to advance manufacturing approaches for next-generation missile components. It is important to be clear about what the announcement does and does not say: the company describes the effort as exploratory. It is an assessment of where its CF3D process could be used, not a production order.

---

## What the Contract Covers

According to the company's announcement, the work is performed in partnership with the U.S. Army's Manufacturing Technology (ManTech) program and executed through America Makes. Continuous Composites is assessing applications for CF3D within the Precision Strike Missile architecture. The components it names are nose cones, fins, leading edges, and bulkheads, along with other structures that need high performance, cost efficiency, and faster delivery.

The stated goals are to improve throughput and yield, reduce variability, and strengthen supply chain resilience, with the aim of establishing a scalable manufacturing approach. The announcement gives no contract value and no duration beyond "multi-year." It does not say that any CF3D part has been qualified or fielded.

"We believe our technology provides game-changing capabilities to the U.S. industrial base," said Steve Starner, CEO of Continuous Composites, in the release.

---

## What CF3D Actually Does

Continuous Composites' CF3D process is different from printing with fiber-filled filament, and the distinction matters technically.

In fiber-filled filament, short fiber strands are blended into the plastic. The company's own description is that those short strands provide limited strength.

CF3D instead embeds long, uninterrupted fiber strands into the part as it is printed. The company says its end effectors steer dry fiber tows (structural fibers such as carbon, glass, and aramid) along complex paths, and that a photopolymer matrix is cured immediately with UV light as the fiber is placed. This means:

- Fiber can be steered along chosen paths rather than cut into short lengths
- Cure happens during deposition, at the point where the fiber is placed
- According to the company, the method avoids the cost of molds and autoclaves

Continuous Composites has worked with Arkema's Sartomer business on UV-curable resins for the process.

---

## The Traditional Composite Baseline

To understand the appeal, it helps to know what CF3D is being compared with. Conventional carbon fiber parts are generally laid up in or on a mold and then cured, often in an autoclave. Every new shape needs its own tooling, so a design change means new tooling as well as new parts.

A process that places and cures fiber directly, without a mold, changes that. A geometry change becomes a change to the programmed fiber path. The Army contract announcement names cost efficiency and faster delivery among the things its target structures need.

---

## Prior Defense Work

The Army contract is not the company's first defense project. In April 2021, Continuous Composites reported completing a two-year contract with the U.S. Air Force Research Laboratory and Lockheed Martin under the Wing Structure Design for Manufacturing (WiSDM) project, which aimed to cut the cost and lead time of Low-Cost Attritable Aircraft wing structures. CF3D was used to manufacture carbon fiber wing spars, and the company reported that the wing assembly reached 160% of design limit load without any damage to the spars.

---

## What to Watch

The announcement describes an assessment, so the open questions are the ones it leaves unanswered: which components, if any, move beyond evaluation, and whether the work leads to qualification or production. No public data from the Army effort is available yet. Until it is, the accurate description of this contract is an exploratory, multi-year effort to find out where CF3D fits in missile manufacturing.
